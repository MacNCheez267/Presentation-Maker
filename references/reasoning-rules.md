# Reasoning Rules

Use this expandable starter taxonomy only for product- or service-focused presentations. It is not a general presentation outline. For lessons, research, reports, strategy, training, and other general presentations, choose a story from [Presentation Structures](presentation-structures/index.md).

Before using the taxonomy, identify the product's primary user problem, audience, purpose, use context, and relevant functional or price-point constraints. Assign each product type to one group based on its main user problem. Treat every entry as a starting recommendation, not a rigid category rule or a claim about every product in that market. Do not duplicate a product type across groups to match multiple industries.

Use the taxonomy to shape a product overview, launch, or demo. Adapt its story pattern to the audience and presentation goal; it is not a mandatory sequence or landing-page template. Include a trial, booking, purchase, signup, or other call to action only when the stated goal and audience make it useful. Otherwise, end with a takeaway, decision, or relevant next step. Recast hero, pricing, and CTA language as presentation content rather than copying a landing-page sequence.

Use product type to suggest a story and possible visual language only. Select palette and typography independently from their standalone references; never let a taxonomy row lock those choices. For each product type, provide:

- **Product story / walkthrough pattern:** a flexible sequence that shows the problem, product experience, evidence or limitations, and a useful decision or next step where appropriate.
- **Style priority:** one coherent dominant visual language and, only when helpful, one restrained supporting direction. Use concrete design qualities.

Add product types only when they represent a distinct, useful user problem and materially improve coverage. Keep the taxonomy focused on story and possible visual language; palette and typography are selected independently. Category conventions can guide familiarity, but avoid stereotypes, unsupported promises, and visual formulas used without a reason. Real-world graphic design examples may inform visual direction; adapt qualities rather than copying work. When a sourced example or factual claim materially informs the recommendation, cite it according to [Research and Sourcing](research-and-sourcing.md).
