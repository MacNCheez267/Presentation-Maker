# Design Guidelines

Use these principles to make a presentation clear, coherent, and easy for its audience to follow. Apply them to the story and to individual slides. Adapt the guidance to the presentation's purpose, audience, delivery format, and supplied requirements; these are decision aids, not a mandatory slide template.

## Build a clear story

A general presentation often includes the following building blocks:

1. **Cover:** State the topic. Add the presenter, organization, or date when useful. Use a relevant visual or restrained design element to set the tone.
2. **Opening:** Give the audience a reason to care with a question, problem, short story, example, or striking fact.
3. **Purpose:** Explain what the presentation will answer, teach, or help the audience decide. Combine this with the opening when a separate slide would feel unnecessary.
4. **Context:** Provide the background the audience needs, without delaying the main point with unrelated detail.
5. **Main points:** Develop the central ideas in an order that makes their relationship clear.
6. **Evidence and examples:** Support claims with accurate data, demonstrations, visuals, or concrete examples.
7. **Implications or application:** Explain what the information means or how the audience can use it, when relevant.
8. **Conclusion:** Reinforce the key takeaway and close the story without introducing a major new idea.
9. **Next step or discussion:** Include a useful action, decision, question period, or reflection when it fits the purpose.
10. **Sources and credits:** Acknowledge research, data, and borrowed visuals in a readable, appropriate place.

These sections do not require one slide each. Combine, omit, or reorder them to fit the brief, audience, and speaking time. Preserve required content and use the relevant story pattern in [Presentation Structures](presentation-structures/index.md). Do not force a call to action or a question slide when it does not serve the presentation.

## Make the message easy to grasp

- Give each slide one primary job and one main takeaway. Make that takeaway visible in the title or first line.
- Establish visual hierarchy with size, weight, contrast, placement, and whitespace. The audience should be able to tell what to notice first and how supporting details relate.
- Keep related items visually grouped. Align labels with the chart, image, or content they explain; use spacing and proximity to show relationships.
- Use plain, concise language. Explain unfamiliar terms and acronyms when first needed.
- Keep only the on-screen text the audience needs to follow. Move spoken detail to narration or notes; do not shrink text to fit an overloaded slide.
- Use evidence and examples that clarify the point. For example, a slide about checkout drop-off is often easier to understand with a labeled funnel and highlighted loss point than with a paragraph listing every stage.
- Use graphics to explain or support the message. A poster-like hero image can establish a product's tone, but it should not obscure the title or key information.

## Use visual design with purpose

- Match visual style to the topic and intended feeling. A calm wellness topic might use generous space and soft imagery; an energetic event might use stronger color and type. Keep the information readable in either style.
- Use a consistent system for typography, color roles, alignment, spacing, and recurring elements. Vary composition when content differs; consistency does not mean forcing every slide into the same layout.
- Use color to organize and emphasize, but never as the only way to distinguish categories, states, or sequence. Pair color with labels, shapes, patterns, or position.
- Choose images, icons, diagrams, and charts for their explanatory value. Avoid generic decoration that competes with the takeaway.
- Keep charts accurate and legible. State the takeaway, label important values and units, use appropriate scales, and cite the source. Refer to [Chart Types](chart-types/index.md) when charts or data displays are used.
- Use animation or transitions only when they clarify sequence, focus attention, or support the intended tone. Avoid motion that distracts or delays comprehension.

## Support audience orientation and confidence

- Make the order of ideas easy to follow. Use section titles, transition language, numbering, or progress cues when they help the audience understand where they are and what comes next.
- Keep navigation and recurring labels predictable. Do not make basic cues change meaning from slide to slide.
- When revealing complex information, show it in a useful sequence and preserve enough context for the audience to understand each step.
- Provide feedback for interactive moments. If an audience poll, demo, or live activity is used, explain what to do, show whether the action was received when possible, and make the next step clear.
- Make recovery possible in live demonstrations. Have a clear route back to the main story if a demo, link, or interactive tool does not work; do not let a failed interaction strand the audience.

## Readability and accessibility

- Use readable type sizes and line spacing for the actual delivery context. Review slides at normal presentation size, not only while zoomed in.
- Keep strong foreground/background contrast, including text placed over imagery. Check actual combinations with an appropriate checker or platform accessibility tool when available; palette-level checks alone do not establish conformance.
- Use clear alignment, safe margins, and intentional whitespace. Keep important content away from slide edges.
- Avoid dense paragraphs, tiny footnotes, long unbreakable headings, unexplained abbreviations, and decorative effects that impair reading.
- Do not rely on color alone. Make meaning available through text, shape, pattern, labels, or another visual cue.
- Provide useful alternative text for meaningful visuals when the output format supports it. If reading order or alternative text cannot be inspected, report the limitation instead of marking the check passed.
- Add captions or source notes where needed, and ensure they remain legible in the final presentation.
- Check line breaks, clipping, alignment, contrast, and image crops after content or font changes. Allow room for longer translated or revised copy; do not solve overflow by shrinking all text.

## Avoid these patterns

Avoid slides where everything competes for attention; tiny or low-contrast text; text over busy images without a readable backing; decorative fonts for long passages; color-only status; vague or unexplained labels; important details hidden in fine print; unresponsive interactive elements; inconsistent visual meanings; repetitive layouts that do not fit the content; needless animation; unsupported chart scales; unlabeled units; cramped edge layouts; stretched or misleading image crops; and decorative elements that do not support the message.

## Review the deck

Before delivery, inspect the available preview or rendered slides and check:

- The audience can identify the purpose, follow the sequence, and understand each slide's main point.
- The deck covers the requested content without avoidable gaps or repetition, and its conclusion reinforces the intended takeaway.
- Evidence, names, dates, figures, units, chart labels, source notes, and credits are accurate and readable.
- Text, meaningful graphics, and layout are readable at the intended presentation size and support accessibility.
- Visual choices are consistent where they should be and varied where the content calls for it.
- Interactive elements, navigation cues, and motion are understandable and purposeful.
- Any checks the available preview cannot support are reported as unverified rather than passed.
